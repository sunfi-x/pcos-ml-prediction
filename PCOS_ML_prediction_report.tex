    \noindent \textbf{Abstract.} Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine disorder affecting 8--13\% of women of reproductive age worldwide, often diagnosed late due to heterogeneous clinical manifestations. We build an end-to-end machine learning pipeline on a public cohort of 541 patients (41 clinical, hormonal, physical, and ultrasound features) and compare nine classifiers under repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation (3 repeats) plus an untouched 20\% held-out test set (109 patients). Our headline model, Stacking Ensemble with correlation-based feature selection, achieves a cross-validated ROC-AUC of 0.956 $\pm$ 0.028 and a held-out test accuracy of 0.927 (ROC-AUC 0.945, 95\% CI [0.886, 0.989]). Beyond accuracy, we demonstrate that (i) a leaky validation protocol inflates accuracy by 3.2 percentage points and F1-score by 9.0 points, (ii) most competitive algorithms are practically indistinguishable, (iii) a questionnaire-only feature tier recovers 93\% of the full model's AUC without lab or ultrasound tests, and (iv) a simple follicle-count clinical rule is a strong baseline that the ML model improves mainly by boosting sensitivity (83.3\% vs 61.1\%, recovering 8 additional cases). Explainable AI (SHAP) reveals follicle counts, skin darkening, and cycle length as primary clinical drivers.

\section{INTRODUCTION}
Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) affects between 8\% and 13\% of women of reproductive age worldwide, standing as the primary cause of anovulatory infertility and a major risk factor for metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease \cite{thakre2020pcos, rotterdam2004}. Clinical diagnosis relies on the 2003 Rotterdam criteria, requiring at least two of three markers: oligo/anovulation, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology on pelvic ultrasound.

Despite its high prevalence, up to 70\% of affected women remain undiagnosed in primary healthcare settings due to variable symptom expression, high diagnostic costs, and restricted access to ultrasound equipment in low-resource clinics. Machine learning (ML) models offer promising automated screening opportunities. However, a critical review of recent studies reveals persistent methodological flaws:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Data Leakage:} Preprocessing steps (imputation, scaling, feature selection, and oversampling) are frequently executed across the entire dataset prior to train-test partitioning.
    \item \textbf{Unaddressed Class Imbalance:} Class imbalance (PCOS vs. non-PCOS ratio of 1:2) is often ignored or overfitted.
    \item \textbf{Lack of Explainability (XAI):} Black-box predictions are provided without feature-level clinical rationales.
    \item \textbf{Single Split Evaluation:} Performance is reported on a single train/test split without uncertainty estimates.
    \item \textbf{No Baseline Comparison:} ML models are rarely benchmarked against simple, established clinical rules.
\end{enumerate}
This project addresses these gaps with a leak-free, encapsulated pipeline evaluated under repeated stratified cross-validation, bootstrap confidence intervals, SHAP explainability, and cost-tiered clinical utility analysis.

\section{DATA AND PREPROCESSING}
We use the Kaggle PCOS dataset (Kottarathil \cite{kottarathil2020pcos}): 541 patients from 10 hospitals in Kerala, India, of whom 177 (32.7\%) have PCOS and 364 do not (Fig.~\ref{fig:eda}a). Row identifiers and an uninformative junk column were removed, text-coded numeric fields (e.g., AMH, $\beta$-HCG) were converted to numbers, and a binary \texttt{Cycle\_Irregular} feature was derived, giving 41 features. Missingness is below 1\% per column (Fig.~\ref{fig:eda}b), and a range check flagged 13 physiologically implausible entries (e.g., FSH $= 5052$, pulse rate $= 13$ bpm). Imputation, scaling, SMOTE, and feature selection are all fitted \emph{inside} the cross-validation pipeline so that no information from validation folds leaks into training.

\subsection{Exploratory findings}
Follicle counts separate the classes best (mean 10.8 vs. 4.6 on the right ovary, Cohen's $d = 1.70$), followed by skin darkening, hair growth, and weight gain ($d \approx 1.0$--$1.1$); see Fig.~\ref{fig:eda}c and Fig.~\ref{fig:correlations}b. Cycle irregularity, fast-food intake, and AMH are also positively associated with PCOS.

\section{METHODOLOGY}
\textbf{Split and validation.} 80\% of the data (432 patients) is used for model selection with 10-fold $\times$ 3-repeat stratified CV; 20\% (109 patients: 73 negative, 36 positive) is held out and touched only once for final reporting (seed 42).

\textbf{Models.} Logistic Regression, Elastic-Net LR, Gaussian Naive Bayes, KNN, SVM (RBF), Random Forest, XGBoost, CatBoost, and a Stacking Ensemble.

\textbf{Feature selection.} Three selectors are compared under identical CV: correlation filter (20 features), Chi-square (15), and RFE (15), plus using all features (Fig.~\ref{fig:correlations}b).

\textbf{Imbalance.} SMOTE inside each fold, compared with class weighting and no balancing in a 3-way ablation.

\textbf{Reliability checks.} Bootstrap 95\% CIs, paired $t$-tests with a practical-significance threshold, nested CV, a 30-split seed sweep, calibration (Brier, ECE), decision-curve analysis, and SHAP explanations.

\section{RESULTS}

\subsection{Model comparison}
The correlation-based feature set gave the best CV score, and Stacking Ensemble ranked first (CV ROC-AUC $0.956 \pm 0.028$, CV accuracy 0.894). Table~\ref{tab:performance} gives held-out test results. Stacking Ensemble reaches accuracy 0.927, precision 0.938, recall 0.833, F1 0.882, and specificity 0.973; Random Forest reaches 0.917 accuracy on this split. The test ROC-AUC of 0.945 has a 95\% bootstrap CI of $[0.886, 0.989]$, i.e. about $\pm 0.05$ because 109 patients are held out.

\subsection{No clearly best algorithm}
Paired $t$-tests across identical folds show that only KNN differs from the best model by a practically meaningful margin; every other gap is below 0.011 AUC, roughly a third of the fold-to-fold standard deviation. Several gaps are statistically significant yet clinically irrelevant (e.g., SVM, $p = 0.0001, \Delta = 0.010$). In a 30-split sweep 8 of 9 models win at least once, with Gaussian NB winning most often (27\%), so the choice of model should not be over-interpreted (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation_rigour}b,c). A 120-second AutoML search found essentially no headroom (+0.0004 test AUC), and nested CV shows a negligible optimism of 0.0017 AUC.

\subsection{The leakage experiment}
The same pipeline was evaluated under two protocols that differ only in whether SMOTE and feature selection run before or inside the CV folds. The leaky protocol reports accuracy 0.924 versus 0.893 for the correct one (+3.2 points), recall +9.8 and F1 +9.0 points (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation_rigour}a). This shows that 98--99\% accuracies reported for this dataset without a stated protocol cannot be taken at face value. SMOTE itself did not inflate metrics: test AUC was 0.935 (no balancing), 0.928 (class weights), and 0.932 (SMOTE).

\section{CLINICAL UTILITY}

\subsection{What does each tier of testing buy?}
Using only the 19 questionnaire features (no clinician, lab, or ultrasound) gives CV AUC 0.887, which is 93\% of the full model. Adding clinic vitals gives no gain, the whole blood panel adds only +0.011, and the ultrasound features add +0.062 (Fig.~\ref{fig:clinical_value}a). This supports a low-cost screening workflow: questionnaire first, ultrasound only for flagged patients.

\subsection{Comparison with a clinical rule}
The Rotterdam follicle rule ($\text{follicles} \ge 12$) has \emph{zero} learned parameters yet reaches CV AUC 0.903, i.e. 94.8\% of the ML model's AUC. Its weakness is sensitivity: it finds 22 of 36 cases (recall 0.611) with 1 false alarm, whereas Stacking Ensemble finds 30 (recall 0.833) with 2 false alarms. The model therefore adds 8 true cases for 1 extra false alarm (Fig.~\ref{fig:clinical_value}b). Decision-curve analysis shows positive net benefit over ``treat all'' and ``treat none'' across plausible thresholds (Fig.~\ref{fig:clinical_value}c).

\subsection{Calibration and subgroups}
The Stacking Ensemble gives the best-calibrated probabilities (Brier 0.081, ECE 0.032), while Gaussian NB is the worst (0.109, 0.100), so a competitive AUC does not guarantee trustworthy probabilities. Subgroup analysis across age and BMI categories confirms consistent diagnostic accuracy (Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness_subgroups}d).

\section{DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS}
Prior papers on this dataset report accuracies of 90.9\% to 99.3\% using a single train/test split and no explainability (Fig.~\ref{fig:lit_comp}). Our 92.7\% test accuracy (CV $89.4 \pm 4.2\%$) is obtained under a protocol that we have shown to be leak-free, with confidence intervals, nested CV, and a held-out set.

\textbf{Limitations:} (1) The dataset is small (541 patients; 109 test set), so confidence intervals are wide. (2) It has no documented treatment history, and hospital-attending patients are not a general population. (3) The data contains no raw ultrasound images, only extracted numerical measurements.

\section{CONCLUSION}
A properly validated Stacking Ensemble predicts PCOS with about 0.956 ROC-AUC and 92.7\% test accuracy using explainable features dominated by follicle count and visible symptoms. The main lessons are methodological: report the validation protocol, since leakage alone can add 3.2 points of accuracy; do not over-read small differences between algorithms; and compare against a simple clinical rule. The model's real contribution over the Rotterdam follicle rule is higher sensitivity (30 vs. 22 of 36 cases), and a questionnaire-first screening strategy retains 93\% of the predictive power at zero financial cost.
