\begin{abstract}
Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) affects 8--13\% of women of reproductive age and is
frequently under-diagnosed due to heterogeneous symptom presentation and limited access to
clinical testing in resource-limited settings.  This study presents a machine-learning-based
screening framework trained on 541 patients from 10 hospitals in Kerala, India (177 PCOS, 364 non-PCOS).
Eight models, comprising seven core classifiers and one stacking ensemble, were evaluated under
30-fold repeated stratified cross-validation with an untouched 20\% held-out test set (109 patients).

prevent data leakage.  The best cross-validated model, Random Forest with a 20-feature correlation-based

Rotterdam diagnostic criteria.  A questionnaire-only model (no lab or ultrasound) recovers 93\% of the full
model's AUC, suggesting a viable low-cost screening pathway.  This framework is developed as an academic
coursework prototype; external clinical validation is required before any real-world deployment.

\section{INTRODUCTION}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Background and Motivation}
Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) represents the leading cause of anovulatory infertility and endocrine
morbidity in women of reproductive age globally, affecting between 8\% and 13\% of women depending on the
diagnostic criteria applied \cite{rotterdam2004}.  Beyond reproductive dysfunction, PCOS is associated
with significant metabolic sequelae, including insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia,
and long-term cardiovascular risks.

Clinical diagnosis in standard practice relies on the 2003 Rotterdam consensus criteria, requiring the
presence of at least two of the following three features: (1)~oligo- or anovulation, (2)~clinical and/or
biochemical signs of hyperandrogenism, and (3)~polycystic ovarian morphology assessed via pelvic ultrasonography.
Despite its prevalence, up to 70\% of affected women remain undiagnosed in primary healthcare settings \cite{zad2024}.
Diagnostic barriers are particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income regions due to variable symptom expression,
high out-of-pocket costs for specialist endocrine assays, and limited access to trained sonographers.  Machine
learning (ML) offers potential for automated, lower-cost decision-support tools that evaluate risk using accessible
questionnaire and anthropometric parameters prior to tertiary clinical referral.

\subsection{Research Gap and Objectives}
Among the papers reviewed for this project using this dataset or comparable clinical cohorts, five recurring
methodological limitations were identified:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Data Leakage:} Preprocessing operations (such as feature scaling, missing value imputation,
        oversampling, and feature selection) are frequently performed on the complete dataset prior to cross-validation,
        allowing validation fold information to contaminate model training.
  \item \textbf{Unaddressed Class Imbalance:} The baseline class imbalance ($\approx 2.06:1$ non-PCOS to PCOS ratio) is
        seldom systematically controlled or analyzed inside cross-validation loops.
  \item \textbf{Single Train/Test Splits:} Performance is commonly reported from a single random train/test split,
        yielding point estimates without statistical confidence intervals or split-variance reporting.
  \item \textbf{Absent Explainability:} Model decisions are predominantly presented as opaque predictions without
        feature-level attribution or clinical interpretation.
  \item \textbf{Lack of Clinical Rule Baselines:} ML algorithms are compared against other algorithms but rarely
        evaluated against established non-parametric clinical decision rules (e.g., Rotterdam diagnostic thresholds).
\end{enumerate}

This study addresses these gaps through five concrete objectives:
(1)~construct a leak-free encapsulated pipeline and empirically measure performance inflation under leaky protocols;
(2)~evaluate seven core classifiers and one stacking ensemble using 30-fold repeated stratified cross-validation
and bootstrap confidence intervals;
(3)~apply SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to provide global and patient-level model interpretability;
(4)~benchmark ML models directly against established clinical decision rules under identical evaluation splits; and
(5)~quantify the marginal discriminative value of progressively complex feature acquisition tiers.

\subsection{Data Cleaning and Quality Control}
Six specific data-quality defect categories were audited and resolved in \texttt{src/data.py}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Text-Typed Numerics:} Two continuous columns contained stray text entries (\texttt{AMH} contained
        the character \texttt{"a"}; \texttt{beta-HCG II} contained \texttt{"1.99."}).  Both were coerced to numeric floats via
        \texttt{pd.to\_numeric(errors="coerce")}, setting invalid entries to NaN.
  \item \textbf{Near-Empty Columns:} The column \texttt{Unnamed: 44} contained only 2 non-null values out of 541 and was dropped.
  \item \textbf{Whitespace Normalisation:} Leading and trailing whitespace in column header strings was stripped.
  \item \textbf{Cycle Recoding:} The raw \texttt{Cycle(R/I)} column used codes 2 (regular) and 4 (irregular). One row contained
        code 5 (undefined).  The column was recoded into a binary indicator \texttt{Cycle\_Irregular} ($2 \to 0$, $4 \to 1$),
        and code 5 was coerced to NaN.
  \item \textbf{Impossible Zero Values:} Cycle length of 0 days and endometrial thickness of 0\,mm represent recording errors rather
        than physiological measurements and were set to NaN.
  \item \textbf{Plausible Range Filtering:} Thirteen extreme values falling outside physiological bounds (e.g., FSH 5052\,mIU/mL,
        LH 2018\,mIU/mL, Vitamin D 6014\,ng/mL, BP systolic 12\,mmHg) were blanked to NaN rather than manually edited, ensuring
        imputation handles them without introducing artificial bias.
\end{itemize}

Missing values (under 1\% per feature) are imputed inside cross-validation folds using median imputation for continuous variables
and mode imputation for nominal and binary indicators.

\subsection{Feature Preprocessing and Feature Selection}
Features are categorized into three pipeline transformer paths:
continuous variables undergo median imputation followed by standardisation (\texttt{StandardScaler});
nominal Blood Group (8 ABO/Rh categories) undergoes mode imputation followed by one-hot encoding;
binary symptom indicators undergo mode imputation and pass through unscaled.

Four feature selection strategies are evaluated under identical cross-validation conditions:
(1)~\textbf{All Features (41)} --- baseline pass-through;
(2)~\textbf{Correlation Filter (20)} --- two-stage selection keeping features with $|\text{corr}(x, y)| \ge 0.10$ and removing
collinear pairs ($r > 0.85$);
(3)~\textbf{$\chi^2$-Top-$k$ (15)} --- top 15 features ranked by chi-square statistic following min-max scaling; and
(4)~\textbf{RFE (15)} --- Recursive Feature Elimination driven by Logistic Regression.
All feature selectors operate strictly inside cross-validation training folds as scikit-learn transformers.

\section{METHODOLOGY}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Pipeline Architecture and Encapsulation}
To guarantee complete isolation between training and validation data, every model is constructed as a single
scikit-learn compatible \texttt{imblearn.Pipeline}:
\[
\textit{Impute} \!\to\! \textit{Scale/Encode} \!\to\! \textit{Select} \!\to\! \textit{SMOTE} \!\to\! \textit{Classify}
\]
When evaluated via \texttt{cross\_val\_score}, scikit-learn fits every intermediate transformer exclusively on the 9
training folds and transforms the 10th validation fold.  The \texttt{imblearn} framework is mandatory because SMOTE
resamples row counts during \texttt{fit} while remaining inactive during \texttt{predict}.

\subsection{Validation Protocol and Experimental Design}
The 541-patient dataset was partitioned into an 80\% training pool (432 patients) and a 20\% held-out test set
(109 patients: 73 non-PCOS, 36 PCOS) using stratified random sampling (seed 42).
All model development, hyperparameter selection, feature selection evaluation, and resampling comparisons were conducted
exclusively on the training pool using **repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation (3 repeats)**, generating 30 validation
scores per model configuration.  The held-out test set was evaluated once at final reporting.
Bootstrap 95\% percentile confidence intervals (2000 resamples) were calculated for all held-out test metrics.
Statistical comparisons across models utilize paired $t$-tests on the 30 paired fold scores with a practical significance threshold
of $\Delta\,\text{AUC} > 0.020$.  Because repeated CV folds overlap, the paired $t$-test serves as a suggestive indicator rather
than an exact independent test.

\subsection{Evaluated Models}
Seven core classifiers and one stacking ensemble were evaluated (Table~\ref{tab:models}).
The Stacking Ensemble incorporates Logistic Regression, Random Forest, SVM (RBF), and XGBoost as base estimators,
coupled with a Logistic Regression meta-learner fitted on 5-fold out-of-fold probability predictions.
\textit{Note: CatBoost was configured as an optional module in the codebase but omitted from the primary benchmark due to execution environment dependencies.}

Minority class oversampling via SMOTE ($k\_\text{neighbors}=5$) was evaluated against class weighting (\texttt{class\_weight="balanced"})

F1-score, specificity, Brier score, and Expected Calibration Error (ECE).

\subsection{Robustness and Probability Calibration}
Nested CV (outer fold selection, inner fold tuning) yielded CV AUC $0.9541 \pm 0.020$ vs flat CV $0.9558$, indicating negligible selection bias ($0.0017$).
AutoML benchmarking using FLAML (120s search budget) converged on a tuned Random Forest with test AUC 0.946 vs hand-built RF 0.945 ($+0.0004$),
confirming that data quality rather than model tuning limits performance.

\section{DISCUSSION}
% ============================================================

\textbf{Methodological Leakage vs Model Performance:}
Protocol differences produce $+2.7$ pp accuracy inflation on this dataset.  The leakage experiment shows high performance can be influenced by methodology.

\textbf{Statistical vs Practical Separability:}
Paired $t$-tests across 30 folds flag minor AUC deficits ($<0.020$) as statistically significant, but these are clinically small.
Among the evaluated models, KNN showed the clearest practically meaningful performance disadvantage relative to Random Forest ($\Delta = -0.028$).

\textbf{Feature Value in Screening:}
FSH, LH, and AMH show Cohen's $d < 0.18$.  The blood-panel tier provided only a small incremental improvement in discrimination relative to the clinic-vitals tier.

\textbf{Literature Comparison Context:}
Among the papers reviewed for this project, reported accuracies span 90.9--99.3\% using single splits (Table~\ref{tab:literature}).
Our 91.7\% test accuracy is obtained under a leak-free 30-fold protocol with bootstrap CIs.

% ============================================================
\section{LIMITATIONS}
% ============================================================

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt,label=\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{Predictor--Label Circularity:} Follicle count is a key SHAP feature and a Rotterdam diagnostic criterion.
\item \textbf{Small Test Sample Size:} With 36 positive test cases, the 95\% CI on recall spans $\pm 12$ pp.
\item \textbf{Hospital Selection Bias:} PCOS prevalence is 32.7\% versus 8--13\% in the general population.
\item \textbf{No External Validation:} Generalisability to independent geographic cohorts is not established.
\item \textbf{Ambiguous Column Semantics:} \texttt{Cycle length(days)} median is 5, suggesting bleeding duration.
\item \textbf{Unblinded Assessment:} Symptom features were recorded by clinicians making the diagnosis.
\end{enumerate}

% ============================================================
\section{ETHICAL AND CLINICAL CONSIDERATIONS}
% ============================================================

This framework is an academic coursework screening prototype and must not be used as a standalone clinical diagnostic system.
PCOS diagnosis requires the Rotterdam criteria applied by a qualified clinician.  Any deployment would require prospective
external validation, regulatory assessment, ethics approval, and compliance with data protection regulations.

% ============================================================
\section{CONCLUSION}
% ============================================================

This study builds a leakage-free ML screening pipeline for PCOS, achieving CV ROC-AUC $0.957 \pm 0.017$ and test AUC $0.945$
[CI: 0.887, 0.991] with Random Forest.  The primary contribution is demonstrating that leaky preprocessing inflates accuracy by $+2.7$ pp.
SHAP analysis aligns predictions with Rotterdam criteria, and the questionnaire-only tier recovers 93\% of full model AUC.
External clinical validation is required before real-world deployment.
