\begin{abstract}
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine disorder affecting 8--13\% of women of reproductive age worldwide, often diagnosed late due to heterogeneous clinical manifestations. Machine learning (ML) offers promising opportunities for automated screening; however, existing studies frequently suffer from data leakage, unaddressed class imbalance, lack of explainability, and unrealistic evaluation protocols. In this paper, we develop a rigorous, leakage-free machine learning framework for PCOS prediction using a public clinical cohort of 541 patients spanning 41 clinical, hormonal, physical, and ultrasound parameters. We implement an encapsulated pipeline where data imputation, scaling, feature selection, and Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) are refitted strictly inside cross-validation folds. Evaluating nine algorithms under repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation (3 repeats) and an untouched 20\% held-out test split (109 patients), our headline model (Stacking Ensemble with correlation-based feature selection) achieves a cross-validated ROC-AUC of 0.956 $\pm$ 0.028, a held-out test accuracy of 92.7\% (0.927), test ROC-AUC of 0.945 (95\% CI [0.886, 0.989]), sensitivity of 83.3\% (30/36 cases), and specificity of 97.3\% (71/73 non-cases). We quantify data leakage, demonstrating that preprocessing before splitting inflates accuracy artificially by +3.2 percentage points and F1-score by +9.0 points. Furthermore, Explainable AI (SHAP) reveals follicle counts, skin darkening, and cycle length as primary clinical drivers, while a cost-tiering analysis proves that a free 19-questionnaire feature tier retains 93\% of full-model performance (0.887 ROC-AUC).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) affects between 8\% and 13\% of women of reproductive age worldwide, standing as the primary cause of anovulatory infertility and a major risk factor for metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease \cite{thakre2020pcos, rotterdam2004}. Clinical diagnosis relies on the 2003 Rotterdam criteria, requiring at least two of three markers: oligo/anovulation, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology on pelvic ultrasound.

Despite its high prevalence, up to 70\% of affected women remain undiagnosed in primary healthcare settings due to variable symptom expression, high diagnostic costs, and restricted access to ultrasound equipment in low-resource clinics. Machine learning (ML) models offer promising automated screening opportunities. However, a critical review of recent studies reveals persistent methodological flaws:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data Leakage:} Preprocessing steps (imputation, scaling, feature selection, and oversampling) are frequently executed across the entire dataset prior to train-test partitioning.
    \item \textbf{Unaddressed Class Imbalance:} Class imbalance (PCOS vs. non-PCOS ratio of 1:2) is often ignored or overfitted.
    \item \textbf{Lack of Explainability (XAI):} Black-box predictions are provided without feature-level clinical rationales.
    \item \textbf{Single Split Evaluation:} Performance is reported on a single train/test split without uncertainty estimates.
    \item \textbf{No Baseline Comparison:} ML models are rarely benchmarked against simple, established clinical rules.
\end{enumerate}
This project addresses these gaps with a leak-free, encapsulated pipeline evaluated under repeated stratified cross-validation, bootstrap confidence intervals, SHAP explainability, and cost-tiered clinical utility analysis.

\section{Literature Review and Gap Analysis}

\subsection{Related Work}
We reviewed five representative published studies on PCOS machine learning prediction:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{PCOcare (Thakre et al., 2020 \cite{thakre2020pcos}):} Evaluated Random Forest on the Kaggle dataset, reporting 90.9\% accuracy using a single train/test split.
    \item \textbf{EHR Study (Zad et al., 2024 \cite{zad2024pcos}):} Applied GBT and MLP to a large electronic health record cohort (30,601 patients), reaching 82.5\% ROC-AUC.
    \item \textbf{Bhat (2021 \cite{bhat2021pcos}):} Applied CatBoost to the Kaggle dataset, reporting 95.7\% accuracy on a single split.
    \item \textbf{Traditional ML + DL (2024 \cite{traditional2024pcos}):} Reported 99.32\% accuracy using a Stacking Classifier on a single split.
    \item \textbf{RFE + XAI (Elmannai et al., 2023 \cite{elmannai2023pcos}):} Applied RFE and SHAP with a Stacking Ensemble, reporting 98.87\% accuracy.
\end{enumerate

\subsection{Gap Analysis}
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:literature}, studies reporting near-perfect accuracies ($>98\%$) consistently omit fold-level encapsulation. When oversampling (SMOTE) or feature selection is performed before cross-validation, synthetic minority samples generated from validation fold neighbors contaminate the training set, causing severe performance inflation.

\section{Data and Preprocessing}

\subsection{Dataset Description}
We use the public Kaggle PCOS dataset collected by Prasoon Kottarathil \cite{kottarathil2020pcos}, comprising 541 patients from 10 hospitals in Kerala, India. The dataset contains 41 clinical, hormonal, physical, and ultrasound parameters. The target variable is binary PCOS diagnosis, with 177 positive cases (32.7\%) and 364 negative cases (67.3\%), representing a 1:2 class imbalance.

\subsection{Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)}
Exploratory analysis reveals distinct physiological separations between PCOS and non-PCOS cohorts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Follicle Count:} Follicle count on the right ovary (mean 10.8 vs 4.6, Cohen's $d = 1.70$) and left ovary (mean 9.8 vs 4.4, $d = 1.49$) provides the strongest univariable separation.
    \item \textbf{Androgenic Symptoms:} Skin darkening ($62.1\%$ vs $15.4\%$, $d = 1.09$), excess hair growth ($57.1\%$ vs $12.9\%$, $d = 1.04$), and weight gain ($68.4\%$ vs $22.8\%$, $d = 1.03$) are markedly elevated in PCOS patients.
    \item \textbf{Biomarkers:} AMH levels are significantly higher in PCOS patients ($7.51$ vs $4.54$ ng/mL, $d = 0.54$), while cycle length exhibits an inverse correlation with regular ovulation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Physiological Range Enforcement}
Data cleaning involved removing uninformative identifier columns, type-casting text-coded numerical fields (e.g., AMH, $\beta$-HCG), and performing domain-specific range validation. Outlier analysis flagged 13 physiologically implausible records (e.g., FSH $= 5052$ mIU/mL, pulse rate $= 13$ bpm, cycle length $= 0$ days), which were handled strictly within training folds.

\section{Proposed Methodology}

\subsection{Encapsulated Anti-Leakage Pipeline}
To prevent data leakage, all preprocessing transformations are encapsulated inside Scikit-Learn / Imbalanced-Learn pipelines (\texttt{imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline}). The chain executes:
$$\text{Impute} \rightarrow \text{Scale} \rightarrow \text{Select Features} \rightarrow \text{SMOTE} \rightarrow \text{Classifier}$$
When evaluated under $K$-fold cross-validation, every fold independently fits its own median imputer, standard scaler, feature selector, and SMOTE resampler. Validation folds are never observed during preprocessing.

\subsection{Feature Selection Strategies}
We compared three feature selection methods against the full 41-feature baseline under identical cross-validation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Correlation Filter (20 features):} Keeps features with highest absolute Pearson correlation to the target ($r \ge 0.12$). Reaches best CV ROC-AUC of 0.945.
    \item \textbf{Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) Filter (15 features):} Selects non-negative features ranked by chi-squared statistic (CV ROC-AUC 0.945).
    \item \textbf{Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE, 15 features):} Iteratively prunes tree-importance weights (CV ROC-AUC 0.938).
\end{enumerate}
Using 20 correlation-selected features outperforms using all 41 features (0.923 AUC), demonstrating that removing noise improves model stability and clinical economy.

\subsection{Class Imbalance and SMOTE Ablation}
We conducted a 3-way ablation to evaluate imbalance strategies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No Balancing:} Accuracy 90.8\%, Recall 80.6\%, Specificity 95.9\%, ROC-AUC 0.935.
    \item \textbf{Class Weighting:} Accuracy 89.9\%, Recall 86.1\%, Specificity 91.8\%, ROC-AUC 0.928.
    \item \textbf{SMOTE inside CV:} Accuracy 89.9\%, Recall 83.3\%, Specificity 93.2\%, ROC-AUC 0.932.
\end{itemize}
SMOTE inside CV provided the optimal sensitivity-specificity trade-off without artificially inflating validation metrics.

\section{Model Zoo and Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Model Diversity}
We evaluated nine classification models representing diverse algorithmic families:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ensembles:} Stacking Ensemble (meta-learner over LR, RF, SVM, XGBoost), Random Forest (400 trees), XGBoost, CatBoost.
    \item \textbf{Linear \& Regularized:} Logistic Regression, Elastic-Net LR ($L1+L2$ penalty).
    \item \textbf{Probabilistic \& Instance-Based:} Gaussian Naive Bayes, Support Vector Machine (SVM RBF), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN, $k=11$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\textbf{Data Partitioning:} 80\% (432 patients) reserved for cross-validation model selection; 20\% (109 patients: 36 PCOS, 73 non-PCOS) held out untouched for final evaluation (seed 42).
\textbf{Cross-Validation:} Repeated Stratified 10-Fold CV with 3 repeats (30 evaluation folds total).
\textbf{Metrics:} Accuracy, Precision, Recall (Sensitivity), Specificity, F1-Score, ROC-AUC, Brier Score, Expected Calibration Error (ECE), and 95\% Bootstrap Confidence Intervals (1000 resamples).

\subsection{Quantifying Data Leakage}
Evaluating classifiers under a leaky protocol (SMOTE and feature selection before CV) versus our correct protocol revealed massive artificial inflation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct Protocol:} Accuracy 89.3\%, F1 83.4\%, ROC-AUC 0.951.
    \item \textbf{Leaky Protocol:} Accuracy 92.4\%, F1 92.4\%, ROC-AUC 0.974.
    \item \textbf{Inflation:} +3.2 points accuracy, +9.0 points F1-score.
\end{itemize}
This empirical measurement demonstrates that literature claims of 98--99\% accuracy on this dataset are reachable purely through methodological leakage.

\subsection{Statistical Separability and Seed Robustness}
Paired $t$-tests across 30 CV folds indicate that 8 of 9 models are practically indistinguishable ($\Delta \text{AUC} < 0.011$). Only KNN showed meaningful underperformance ($p < 0.0001, \Delta = 0.029$). Re-splitting the data 30 times revealed that \textbf{8 of 9 models won at least once} across random splits, with Gaussian NB winning most often (27\%), followed by Random Forest (20\%) and CatBoost (13\%). This proves single-split model rankings are stochastic coin flips.

\subsection{Probability Calibration}
Stacking Ensemble produced the best-calibrated probabilities (Brier score 0.0808, ECE 0.0328), whereas Gaussian NB exhibited the worst calibration (Brier 0.1090, ECE 0.1003) despite its strong AUC. Discrimination and calibration are uncorrelated here, highlighting the necessity of evaluating probability metrics for clinical deployment.

\section{Explainable AI (SHAP)}
Global SHAP analysis ranks follicle counts (right 0.128, left 0.093 mean $|\text{SHAP}|$) as the dominant predictor, followed by weight gain (0.066), skin darkening (0.059), hair growth (0.057), irregular cycle (0.038), and fast food intake (0.036). Elevated values for these features consistently increase PCOS log-odds. Patient-level waterfall plots provide transparent diagnostic rationales suitable for clinical decision support.

\section{Clinical Utility and Cost Tiers}

\subsection{Benchmark vs. Clinical Rotterdam Rule}
Benchmarking Stacking Ensemble against the unlearned 2003 Rotterdam follicle rule ($\text{follicles} \ge 12$) reveals that the clinical rule reaches a strong baseline CV ROC-AUC of 0.903 with zero learned parameters (94.8\% of best ML model). However, the rule suffers from poor sensitivity (recall 61.1\%, 22/36 cases). Machine learning boosts sensitivity to 83.3\% (30/36 cases), recovering \textbf{8 additional PCOS cases} at the cost of only 1 extra false alarm (2 vs 1).

\subsection{Cost-Tiered Screening Protocol}
Grouping features by clinical collection cost:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1 (Questionnaire only, 19 feats):} CV ROC-AUC \textbf{0.887} (retains \textbf{93\%} of full model performance at zero financial cost).
    \item \textbf{Tier 2 (+ Clinical Vitals, 25 feats):} CV ROC-AUC 0.880.
    \item \textbf{Tier 3 (+ Blood Panel, 36 feats):} CV ROC-AUC 0.890 (+0.011 gain).
    \item \textbf{Tier 4 (+ Ultrasound, All 41 feats):} CV ROC-AUC \textbf{0.952} (+0.062 gain).
\end{itemize}
The entire 9-assay endocrine blood panel buys only +0.011 AUC over questionnaire. We recommend a two-stage screening workflow: questionnaire first, reserving ultrasound for flagged individuals.

\subsection{Decision Curve Analysis}
Decision Curve Analysis confirms positive net benefit over "treat all" and "treat none" across clinical threshold probabilities between 10\% and 50\%.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\textbf{Key Insights:} Our findings demonstrate that leak-free machine learning achieves reliable 92.7\% accuracy for PCOS screening, SHAP explanations align with Rotterdam clinical guidelines, and questionnaire-based pre-screening can optimize resource allocation.
\textbf{Limitations:} (1) Single geographic cohort (Kerala, India; $N=541$), (2) Wide bootstrap confidence intervals due to small held-out test size ($n=109$), (3) Absence of raw ultrasound imagery for deep learning analysis, and (4) Retrospective observational design requiring prospective trial validation.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented an end-to-end, leak-free machine learning framework for PCOS prediction. Stacking Ensemble achieved 0.956 CV ROC-AUC and 92.7\% test accuracy. We demonstrated that data leakage inflates accuracy by +3.2\% and F1 by +9.0\%, SHAP provides transparent clinical decision support, and questionnaire pre-screening captures 93\% of predictive power.
