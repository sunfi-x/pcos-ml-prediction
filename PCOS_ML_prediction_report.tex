\begin{abstract}
Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most prevalent endocrine disorders affecting women of reproductive age worldwide, often characterized by delayed diagnosis due to heterogeneous clinical manifestations. Machine learning (ML) offers promising automated screening opportunities; however, existing studies often suffer from data leakage, unaddressed class imbalance, lack of explainability, and unrealistic evaluation protocols. In this paper, we develop a rigorous, leakage-free machine learning framework for PCOS prediction using a public clinical dataset of 541 patients spanning 41 clinical, hormonal, and physical measurements. We implement an encapsulated pipeline where data cleaning, range enforcement, feature selection, and Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) are refitted strictly inside cross-validation folds. Evaluating nine algorithms under repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation and an untouched 20\% held-out test split, our headline model (Stacking Ensemble / Random Forest) achieves a cross-validated ROC-AUC of 0.956 $\pm$ 0.028, a held-out test accuracy of 92.7\% (0.927), test ROC-AUC of 0.945 (95\% CI [0.887, 0.991]), sensitivity of 83.3\% (30/36 cases), and specificity of 97.3\% (71/73 non-cases). We quantify data leakage, demonstrating that preprocessing before splitting inflates accuracy artificially by +3.2 percentage points and F1-score by +9.0\%. Furthermore, Explainable AI (SHAP) reveals follicle counts, skin darkening, and cycle irregular length as key clinical signals, while a cost-tiering analysis proves that a free 19-questionnaire feature tier retains 93\% of full-model performance (0.887 ROC-AUC).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) affects between 8\% and 13\% of reproductive-aged women globally, standing as the leading cause of female anovulatory infertility and a major risk factor for type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease \cite{thakre2020pcos}. Clinical diagnosis relies predominantly on the 2003 Rotterdam Criteria, requiring at least two of three features: oligo/anovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology on pelvic ultrasound.

Despite its high prevalence, up to 70\% of affected women remain undiagnosed in primary healthcare settings due to variable symptom expression, high diagnostic costs, and restricted access to ultrasound equipment in low-resource clinics. Machine learning (ML) models have emerged as potential diagnostic assistance systems. However, a critical review of existing ML studies reveals persistent methodological flaws:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data Leakage:} Preprocessing steps (imputation, scaling, feature selection, and oversampling) are frequently executed across the entire dataset prior to train-test partitioning.
    \item \textbf{Unaddressed Class Imbalance:} Synthetic oversampling is performed without cross-validation bounds, yielding overly optimistic evaluation metrics.
    \item \textbf{Black-Box Models:} Lack of interpretable AI mechanisms inhibits clinician trust and adoption.
    \item \textbf{Absence of Clinical Baselines:} Models are compared exclusively against each other rather than against standard clinical decision rules.
\end{enumerate}

To address these gaps, this study presents a fully reproducible, leakage-free ML framework. Our key contributions include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Designing an anti-leakage pipeline architecture where all fitting operations occur strictly within cross-validation training folds.
    \item Conducting a controlled leakage experiment quantifying exact accuracy inflation caused by premature preprocessing.
    \item Applying SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to demystify feature importance and patient-level risk predictions.
    \item Evaluating a 4-tier cost-based deployment analysis demonstrating the predictive utility of zero-cost questionnaire features.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review \& Gap Analysis}
We systematically evaluated five representative recent studies predicting PCOS using machine learning algorithms:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Paper 1 (Thakre et al., 2020) \cite{thakre2020pcos}:} Evaluated Random Forest, Logistic Regression, SVM, KNN, and Naive Bayes on the Kaggle 541-patient dataset, reporting Random Forest as top with 90.9\% accuracy on a single split.
    \item \textbf{Paper 2 (Zad et al., 2024) \cite{zad2024predicting}:} Utilized a large EHR dataset (n=30,601) from Boston Medical Center, reporting AUC scores between 0.80 and 0.85 using Gradient Boosted Trees.
    \item \textbf{Paper 3 (Bhat, 2021) \cite{bhat2021detection}:} Applied CatBoost on 541 instances, reporting 95.7\% accuracy without detailing imbalance handling or protocol containment.
    \item \textbf{Paper 4 (Traditional ML + DL, 2024):} Tested deep sequence models and Stacking Classifiers, claiming 99.32\% accuracy on 541 instances using a single train-test split.
    \item \textbf{Paper 5 (Elmannai et al., 2023) \cite{elmannai2023pcos}:} Combined RFE feature selection and Stacking Ensembles with XAI, reporting 98.87\% accuracy.
\end{enumerate}

Our gap analysis identifies five critical flaws across prior literature:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gap 1 (Unaddressed Class Imbalance):} 4 of 5 studies fail to explain how class balance (364 non-PCOS vs 177 PCOS) was maintained.
    \item \textbf{Gap 2 (Pervasive Data Leakage):} High accuracy claims (98--99\%) on small samples (n=541) strongly indicate preprocessing over train and validation sets simultaneously.
    \item \textbf{Gap 3 (Lack of Explainability):} Only 1 of 5 papers incorporated explainable AI to validate clinical feature logic.
    \item \textbf{Gap 4 (Single Split Instability):} Reporting results from a single 80/20 partition ignores high variance inherent in small patient samples.
    \item \textbf{Gap 5 (No Baseline Comparison):} Models were never benchmarked against published clinical rules like the Rotterdam Criteria.
\end{itemize}

\section{Dataset \& Data Preprocessing}
The study utilizes the publicly available PCOS dataset collected across 10 hospitals in Kerala, India (Prasoon Kottarathil, Kaggle). It comprises 541 female patients (364 Non-PCOS, 177 PCOS; class ratio 2.06:1) and 41 predictors spanning clinical symptoms, physical vital signs, blood hormone panels, and pelvic ultrasound measurements.

\subsection{Data Cleaning \& Range Enforcement}
Raw clinical workbooks frequently contain typographical and measurement errors. We executed strict data cleaning steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Type Coercion:} Text-encoded numeric fields (e.g. \texttt{II beta-HCG} containing stray characters like \texttt{"1.99."}) were coerced to numeric floats.
    \item \textbf{Physiological Range Enforcement:} Plausible range bounds were established based on medical norms (e.g. Systolic BP: 70--250 mmHg, Diastolic BP: 40--150 mmHg, FSH/LH: 0.1--200 mIU/mL). Out-of-bounds values (such as blood pressure recorded as 12/80 or hormone values exceeding 5000) were converted to \texttt{NaN} rather than manually guessed.
    \item \textbf{Fold-Encapsulated Imputation:} Missing values (18 total cells out of 22,181) were median-imputed strictly using training fold statistics.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Proposed Methodology \& Pipeline Architecture}

To ensure complete methodological containment, the entire learning procedure is implemented using an encapsulated pipeline structure using \texttt{scikit-learn} and \texttt{imbalanced-learn}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{reports/figures/09_feature_set_comparison.png}
\caption{Cross-validated comparison across feature selection strategies.}
\label{fig:feature_selection}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Feature Selection Strategies}
We evaluated three feature selection transformers fitted inside cross-validation folds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correlation-Based Selection:} Removes highly collinear predictors ($|r| > 0.85$) while retaining features with strong target correlation, yielding \textbf{20 features}.
    \item \textbf{Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) Selection:} Selects top $k=15$ non-negative features.
    \item \textbf{Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE):} Uses Random Forest estimator to prune down to $k=15$ features.
\end{itemize}

As shown in Fig. \ref{fig:feature_selection}, Correlation-based selection achieved the highest mean CV ROC-AUC (0.945), outperforming the raw 41-feature baseline (0.923) by reducing feature space noise.

\subsection{Leakage-Free Class Balancing (SMOTE)}
Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) interpolates synthetic minority class samples between $k$-nearest neighbors. To prevent data leakage, SMOTE is applied \textbf{only to the training fold} during each cross-validation step. Validation and test folds remain completely untouched and un-sampled.

\section{Experimental Setup \& Model Zoo}

\subsection{Data Partitioning \& Cross-Validation}
The dataset (n=541) was partitioned into:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training Set (80\%):} 432 patients (291 Non-PCOS, 141 PCOS) used for feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, and cross-validation.
    \item \textbf{Held-Out Test Set (20\%):} 109 patients (73 Non-PCOS, 36 PCOS) held untouched until final reporting.
\end{itemize}
Internal validation was conducted using Repeated Stratified 10-Fold Cross-Validation (3 repeats, total 30 folds).

\subsection{Model Zoo}
We benchmarked nine diverse learning algorithms: Logistic Regression, Elastic-Net Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM RBF), Gaussian Naive Bayes, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest, XGBoost, CatBoost, and a Stacking Ensemble (combining RF, XGBoost, LR with Logistic Regression meta-learner).

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Models were evaluated across discrimination (Accuracy, Precision, Recall/Sensitivity, Specificity, F1-Score, ROC-AUC), calibration (Brier Score, ECE), and non-parametric 95\% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals (2,000 resamples).

\subsection{Quantifying Data Leakage Inflation}
To test how much accuracy improper validation invents, we conducted a controlled experiment comparing two protocols on identical data folds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct Protocol:} Selection and SMOTE fitted inside CV. Accuracy = 89.3\%, F1 = 83.4\%.
    \item \textbf{Leaky Protocol:} Selection and SMOTE fitted before CV. Accuracy = 92.4\%, F1 = 92.4\%.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{reports/figures/13_leakage_experiment.png}
\caption{Data leakage experiment demonstrating artificial metric inflation.}
\label{fig:leakage}
\end{figure}

Premature preprocessing causes an artificial inflation of \textbf{+3.2 percentage points in accuracy} and \textbf{+9.0\% in F1-score} (Fig. \ref{fig:leakage}). Synthetic minority samples created before splitting cause validation folds to contain points derived from training instances.

\subsection{Statistical vs. Practical Separability}
Paired t-tests across 30 CV folds (Fig. \ref{fig:significance}) revealed that 7 of 8 models are statistically indistinguishable from the top model (ROC-AUC differences $< 0.010$). Across 30 random train-test splits (Fig. \ref{fig:seed_sweep}), 8 different algorithms won at least once, proving that declaring a "single winning algorithm" from one split is reporting random split noise.

\section{Discussion \& Limitations}

\subsection{Discussion of Findings}
Our findings demonstrate that machine learning models provide significant value in optimizing decision thresholds over rigid clinical rules and enabling low-cost screening. However, reporting unvalidated 98--99\% accuracy numbers damages clinical credibility. Strict pipeline encapsulation is mandatory to yield trustworthy medical AI.

\subsection{Study Limitations}
We acknowledge four inherent study limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sample Size Imprecision:} The held-out test set contains 36 PCOS positive cases, resulting in wider bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals ([70\%, 94\%] for recall).
    \item \textbf{Unblinded Labeling:} Diagnostic labels were assigned by clinicians who recorded the predictors, introducing potential circularity.
    \item \textbf{Absence of Raw Ultrasound Images:} Dataset records numerical follicle counts extracted from scans rather than raw ultrasound imagery.
    \item \textbf{Scope Boundary:} Designed as a screening triage tool, not a diagnostic replacement for specialized clinical evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
This study presented a leak-free, explainable, and cost-aware machine learning framework for PCOS prediction. By enforcing strict fold-level containment for preprocessing, SMOTE, and feature selection, we demonstrated that a realistic 92.7\% accuracy and 0.945 ROC-AUC can be achieved on unseen test data. SHAP interpretations confirmed clinical alignment with Rotterdam criteria, while cost-tiering proved that a free questionnaire model (0.887 AUC) offers an effective triage tool for resource-limited healthcare settings.
