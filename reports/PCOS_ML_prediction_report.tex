  \begin{abstract}
  \noindent \textbf{Abstract.} Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine disorder that is often diagnosed late. We build an end-to-end machine learning pipeline on a public cohort of 541 patients (41 clinical, hormonal and ultrasound features) and compare eight classifiers under repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation plus an untouched 20\% test set. The best model, Random Forest with correlation-based feature selection, reaches a cross-validated ROC-AUC of 0.957 and a held-out test accuracy of 0.917 (ROC-AUC 0.945, 95\% CI [0.887, 0.991]). Beyond accuracy, we show that (i) a leaky validation protocol inflates accuracy by 2.7 points, (ii) most algorithms are practically indistinguishable, (iii) a questionnaire alone recovers 93\% of the full model's AUC, and (iv) a simple follicle-count rule is a strong baseline that the model improves mainly in recall. SHAP is used to explain the predictions.
  \end{abstract}
  \vspace{1.5em}
]

\section{1. INTRODUCTION}
PCOS affects a large share of women of reproductive age and is diagnosed with the Rotterdam criteria (irregular ovulation, hyperandrogenism, polycystic ovaries on ultrasound). Several recent papers report 98--99\% accuracy for ML-based PCOS detection on the same small public dataset, yet most do not describe how class imbalance, feature selection and validation were handled. Our literature review of five such papers found recurring gaps: unaddressed class imbalance, possible leakage, a single train/test split, little explainability, no uncertainty estimates, and no comparison with simple clinical rules. This project closes those gaps with a carefully validated and explainable pipeline.

\section{2. DATA AND PREPROCESSING}
We use the Kaggle PCOS dataset (Kottarathil): 541 patients from 10 hospitals in Kerala, India, of whom 177 (32.7\%) have PCOS and 364 do not (Fig. 1a). Row identifiers and an almost empty junk column were removed, text-coded numeric fields (e.g. AMH, $\beta$-HCG) were converted to numbers, and a binary \texttt{Cycle\_Irregular} feature was derived, giving 41 features. Missingness is below 1\% per column (Fig. 1b), and a range check flagged 13 physiologically implausible entries (e.g. FSH = 5052, pulse rate = 13 bpm). Imputation, scaling, SMOTE and feature selection are all fitted inside the cross-validation pipeline so that no information from validation folds leaks into training.

\subsection{2.1 Exploratory findings}
Follicle counts separate the classes best (mean 10.8 vs. 4.6 on the right ovary, Cohen's $d = 1.70$), followed by skin darkening, hair growth and weight gain ($d \approx 1.0$--$1.1$); see Fig. 1c and Fig. 2. Cycle irregularity, fast-food intake and AMH are also positively associated with PCOS.

\section{3. METHODOLOGY}
\textbf{Split and validation.} 80\% of the data (432 patients) is used for model selection with 10-fold $\times$ 3-repeat stratified CV; 20\% (109 patients: 73 negative, 36 positive) is held out and touched only once for final reporting (seed 42).

\textbf{Models.} Logistic Regression, Elastic-Net LR, Gaussian Naive Bayes, KNN, SVM (RBF), Random Forest, XGBoost and a Stacking Ensemble.

\textbf{Feature selection.} Three selectors are compared under identical CV: correlation filter (20 features), Chi-square (15) and RFE (15), plus using all features.

\textbf{Imbalance.} SMOTE inside each fold, compared with class weighting and no balancing in a 3-way ablation.

\section{4. RESULTS}

\subsection{4.1 Model comparison}
The correlation-based feature set gave the best CV score, and Random Forest ranked first (CV ROC-AUC $0.957 \pm 0.017$, CV accuracy 0.891). Table 1 gives held-out test results. Random Forest reaches accuracy 0.917, precision 0.909, recall 0.833, F1 0.870 and specificity 0.959; XGBoost and the Stacking Ensemble reach 0.927 accuracy on this split. The test ROC-AUC of 0.945 has a 95\% bootstrap CI of $[0.887, 0.991]$, i.e. about $\pm 0.05$ because only 109 patients are held out.

\subsection{4.2 No clearly best algorithm}
Paired $t$-tests across identical folds show that only KNN differs from the best model by a practically meaningful margin; every other gap is below 0.011 AUC, roughly a third of the fold-to-fold standard deviation. Several gaps are statistically significant yet clinically irrelevant (e.g. SVM, $p = 0.028$, $\Delta = 0.017$). In a 30-split sweep four different models win at least once, with Gaussian NB winning most often (40\%), so the choice of model should not be over-interpreted (Fig. 4b,c). A 120-second AutoML search found essentially no headroom (+0.0004 test AUC), and nested CV shows a negligible optimism of 0.0017 AUC. The learning curve plateaus at about 0.956 AUC, so more data of the same kind would help little (Fig. 8).

\subsection{4.3 The leakage experiment}
The same Random Forest was evaluated under two protocols that differ only in whether SMOTE and feature selection run before or inside the CV folds. The leaky protocol reports accuracy 0.927 versus 0.900 for the correct one (+2.7 points), recall +9.8 and F1 +8.3 points (Fig. 4a). This shows that 98--99\% accuracies reported for this dataset without a stated protocol cannot be taken at face value. SMOTE itself did not help: test AUC was 0.935 (no balancing), 0.928 (class weights) and 0.932 (SMOTE).

\subsection{4.4 Explainability (SHAP)}
SHAP ranks follicle counts (right 0.128, left 0.093 mean $|\text{SHAP}|$) first, followed by weight gain, skin darkening, hair growth, irregular cycle and fast-food intake. All of these push the prediction toward PCOS when high, while longer cycle length and higher age push toward non-PCOS (Fig. 5). The waterfall plot explains an individual patient, which is what a clinician actually needs.

\subsection{4.5 What does each tier of testing buy?}
Using only the 19 questionnaire features (no clinician, lab or ultrasound) gives CV AUC 0.887, which is 93\% of the full model. Adding clinic vitals gives no gain, the whole blood panel adds only +0.010, and the ultrasound features add +0.062 (Fig. 6a). This supports a low-cost screening workflow: questionnaire first, ultrasound only for flagged patients.

\subsection{4.6 Comparison with a clinical rule}
The Rotterdam follicle rule ($\text{follicles} \ge 12$) has \emph{zero} learned parameters yet reaches CV AUC 0.902, i.e. 94\% of the ML model's AUC. Its weakness is sensitivity: it finds 22 of 36 cases (recall 0.611) with 1 false alarm, whereas the Random Forest finds 30 (recall 0.833) with 3 false alarms. The model therefore adds 8 true cases for 2 extra false alarms (Fig. 6b). Decision-curve analysis shows positive net benefit over ``treat all'' and ``treat none'' across most plausible thresholds (Fig. 6c); at a 20\% threshold the net benefit is 0.264 versus 0.163 for referring everyone.

\subsection{4.7 Calibration and subgroups}
The Stacking Ensemble gives the best-calibrated probabilities (Brier 0.081, ECE 0.032), while Gaussian NB is the worst (0.109, 0.100), so a competitive AUC does not guarantee trustworthy probabilities. Accuracy across reliable age and BMI subgroups ranges from 0.88 to 0.97, with recall between 0.77 and 0.92; groups with fewer than 10 patients are flagged unreliable (Fig. 8d). Supplementary exploratory and threshold plots are shown in Fig. 9.

\section{5. COMPARISON WITH PRIOR WORK}
Prior papers on this dataset report accuracies of 90.9\% to 99.3\% using a single train/test split and, with one exception, no explainability (Fig. 7c). Our 91.7\% test accuracy (CV $89.1 \pm 2.5\%$) is lower than the 98--99\% claims, but it is obtained under a protocol that we have shown to be leak-free, with confidence intervals, nested CV and a held-out set.

\section{6. LIMITATIONS}
(1) The dataset is small (541 patients; only 109 in the test set), so confidence intervals are wide. (2) It has no documented collection period, eligibility criteria or treatment history, and hospital-attending patients are not a general population. (3) External validation is structurally limited: an independent cohort shares only 8 features with this dataset, so it can validate only a restricted model. (4) The data contains no ultrasound images, only extracted measurements. (5) Some entries are implausible and were only flagged, not corrected.

\section{7. CONCLUSION}
A properly validated Random Forest predicts PCOS with about 0.95 ROC-AUC and 92\% test accuracy using explainable features dominated by follicle count and visible symptoms. The main lessons are methodological: report the validation protocol, since leakage alone can add 2--3 points of accuracy; do not over-read small differences between algorithms; and compare against a simple clinical rule. The model's real contribution over the Rotterdam follicle rule is higher sensitivity (30 vs. 22 of 36 cases), and a questionnaire-first screening strategy retains most of the predictive power at very low cost. Future work should obtain a larger multi-centre cohort with matching features for true external validation.
